\begin{abstract}
Parameter decomposition methods aim to express a trained network as a sum of simpler subcomponents that
are faithful to the model's weights, with each subcomponent corresponding to a distinct mechanism.
Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (SPD) does so by constraining each subcomponent to its simplest,
rank one form and learning an importance assignment that decides which subcomponents are responsible for
a given input. Since the subcomponents are constrained to be rank one, higher dimensional
features can not be captured during training and must instead be reassembled by clustering
multiple lower rank subcomponents together post training.

To explore whether this grouping can instead be done during training, the SPD architecture is adapted
to use rank-$k$ subcomponents together with a relational importance assignment that computes importance across subcomponents rather than independently.

This reformulation while producing sparser importance assignments, appears to be a poor
guide to the decomposition underneath. In this setting a one-hot importance assignment does not imply complete ownership,
since the subcomponents it marks as unimportant still carry relevant magnitude that contribute to the
output. It is concluded that, under this reformulation, the importance values can not be taken
as literal measures of feature ownership, and that other approaches should be considered if higher-dimensional features are to
be captured during training.

\section{Introduction (mangler rettelser, ellers done)}

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to reverse-engineer neural networks into a set of
human interpretable mechanisms. Different branches of the field pursue this goal with different
focal points, either by disentangling directions in activation space, discovering computational subgraphs, or
decomposing the network's parameters directly.

However, both the atomic structure for decomposition
 and the decomposition itself remain open problems.
\autocite{sharkey2025openproblemsmechanisticinterpretability}
Whether a decomposition recovers the computational primitives
of a neural network depends on what the ground truth decomposition
is taken to be. With models consisting of independent features,
the decomposition is unambiguous, with one atom per feature.
If the features are dependent or co-occur, the decomposition becomes underdetermined.
Decompositions that split features across multiple atoms, risk losing the structural information, that gives each feature its meaning,
while decompositions that group multiple features into a single atom, risk losing granularity by collapsing distinct features into a single unit.

For example, a network computing the volume of a cube can be
decomposed into subcomponents corresponding to individual side
lengths, or into a single subcomponent computing the volume directly.
The former corresponds to a feature-level decomposition,
with one atom per input direction, exposing internal
structure but leaving grouping implicit, only recoverable
through co-activation patterns. The latter corresponds
to a structural-level decomposition, with one atom per
computation, exposing the grouping directly but collapsing per side-length information.

% Recently, 
% a formal definition of irreducibility was introduced to disambiguate cases such
% as the cube example.
% \autocite{engels2025languagemodelfeaturesonedimensionally}
% Features are irreducible, and thus constitute units of decomposition,
% if their joint distribution can not be decomposed into independent
% factors or into a mixture of mutually exclusive events.

Beyond what the atoms should be, the decomposition procedure itself remains open.
Recent work focuses on decomposing parameters, as opposed to the activations.
These methods aim to recover subcomponents that are faithful to the original weights, sparse per input, and individually simple. 
One such approach, Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (SPD) \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}, factorizes networks into rank one subcomponents with a learned per subcomponent importance
function.

This work examines SPD in settings with multi-dimensional
features that can not be captured by a single rank one subcomponent,
to see whether they can be recovered during training rather than
through post-hoc clustering.
To
explore this, the subcomponents are extended to rank k and a joint, relational importance function
is introduced, computed across subcomponents rather than independently. 
Lastly, a new toy model, the Toy
Model of Geometry, is introduced to evaluate both SPD and the modification in a higher-dimensional setting.

Across the toy models studied, the relational importance function, implemented as a transformer
block with Softmax over subcomponents, recovers sparse, one-hot causal importance
assignments. These sparse assignments should correspond to full ownership, but the marked
subcomponents, despite directional alignment, only partially reconstruct the feature magnitude
themselves, with inactive subcomponents driving the remainder.

Secondly, in The Toy Model of Superposition it is found
that constraining a subcomponent to rank one does not restrict
it to a single input. When two inputs are sampled together,
the model collapses them onto a shared direction in its hidden space,
and a single rank one subcomponent then captures both,
just as it would a single independent input. rank one therefore
does not appear to indicate the simplest decomposition of the inputs,
since what a rank one subcomponent captures depends on the model
transformation, and specifically on how it has collapsed those
inputs in its hidden space, rather than on the input distribution
in isolation.

Finally, The Toy Model of Geometry shows that the rank-$k$ extension does not translate to SPD
without further modification, as increasing the rank produces the opposite of the intended effect.
Rather than allowing a single subcomponent to absorb an entire feature, the added capacity is used
to share several features across fewer subcomponents, entangling computations that should remain
separate.


\section{Related work (Done to rettelser)}
\subsection{The linear representation hypothesis}
A widespread view in mechanistic interpretability is the linear
representation hypothesis: high-level concepts are encoded as directions
in a network's representation space \autocite{park2024linearrepresentationhypothesisgeometry}.
Together with the superposition hypothesis \autocite{elhage2022toymodelssuperposition},
it forms a central tenet of interpretability, that each feature corresponds
to a single direction, and a hidden state is a sparse linear
combination of feature directions.

Recent work questions how strictly this view should be taken,
distinguishing between a weak form of the hypothesis,
that \textit{some} features are linear, from a stronger form that
\textit{all} features are linear \autocite{mendel2024sae,smith2024strong}. 
Empirically multiple counterexamples to
the stronger form have been proposed, including recurrent
networks that encode token position by magnitude
\autocite{csordás2024recurrentneuralnetworkslearn}, transformers
representing cyclical concepts such as days of the week
\autocite{engels2025languagemodelfeaturesonedimensionally} or modular
arithmetic \autocite{nanda2023progressmeasuresgrokkingmechanistic} in
irreducible low-dimensional subspaces.
\subsection{Activation-space decomposition}
Decomposing a network's activations  focuses on disentangling layer activations into interpretable features.
Here, sparse autoencoders (SAE) are one of the most widely researched methods \autocite{sparseautoencodershighlyinterpretable, bricken2023monosemanticity},
that learn an overcomplete dictionary that expresses each hidden state as a sparse combination of one-dimensional features.
However, these features are not necessarily aligned with the network's computation, due to feature absorption, inconsistencies across dictionary sizes, and a failure to capture feature geometry.
 \autocite{absorptionstudyingfeaturesplitting, sparseautoencoderscanonicalunits,mendel2024sae}.

Circuit discovery extends feature analysis to identify
how a computation is processed by the network's structure,
by identifying  minimal subgraphs required to perform a given task,
without loss of precision. \autocite{olah2020zoomin,marks2025sparsefeaturecircuitsdiscovering}.
However, these methods generally do not account for the
polysemantic and densely connected structure of neural networks,
where the functional behaviour can be attributed to multiple distinct but functionally equivalent subgraphs \autocite{méloux2025everythingeverywhereoncemechanistic}.

\subsection{Parameter-space decomposition}

Recently a newer line of work aims to decompose the network's parameters directly, instead of the activations.
Transcoders \autocite{dunefsky2024transcodersinterpretablellmfeature} represent an intermediate step that learns a sparse approximation of an MLP layer. Parameter decomposition methods go further, factorizing the network's weights directly, with the goal of recovering the network's computational primitives.

Most directly relevant to this work is the description-length-driven
line of Attribution-based Parameter Decomposition
\autocite{AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition} and
Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (SPD)
\autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}, which has
recently been explored in small transformer models
\autocite{christensen2025decompositionsmalltransformermodels}
and cross-layer clustering \autocite{seshadri2026crosslayer}.
While APD decomposes a network into multidimensional
subcomponents,
the method is sensitive to hyperparameters, and does not scale beyond toy settings due
to computational complexity. SPD addresses this
by decomposing each weight matrix into rank one
subcomponents and learning a per-input importance
function over them, at the cost of requiring post-hoc
clustering to recover higher-rank mechanisms;
both methods are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:spd_background}.

In concurrent work, adVersarial Parameter Decomposition (VPD)
\autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd} extends this line of
research by replacing SPD's stochastic masking procedure with an
adversarial objective that searches for subcomponent ablations capable
of invalidating the learned importance assignments. The method enforces
that, for any input, every subset of subcomponents containing those
identified as causally important must still reconstruct the network's
output. VPD also scales parameter decomposition to a small
language model.

\section{Method (done until korrektur)}

\subsection{Background on SPD}
\label{sec:spd_background}
Attribution-based Parameter Decomposition (APD) \autocite{AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition}
and Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (SPD) \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition} both aim to decompose
a network's parameters into a set of parameter components that are faithful
to the original weights, minimal in the number required per input,
and individually as simple as possible.

To formalize this, consider a neural network $f(x, W)$ with weight matrices $W = \{W^{1}, \dots, W^L\}$.
A set of parameter components are said to comprise the network's mechanisms if they satisfy the following properties:
\renewcommand\labelitemi{\tiny$\bullet$}
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0em}
    \item \textbf{Faithfulness}: The parameter components should sum to the 
          parameters of the original network.
    \item \textbf{Minimality}: As few parameter components as possible should 
          be required to replicate the network's output on any given input.
    \item \textbf{Simplicity}: Each component should span as few weight 
          matrices and as few ranks as possible.
\end{itemize}

APD \autocite{AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition} approaches this by decomposing
the network into a set of parameter components without constraining
their rank, using a gradient-based attribution that selects the top-$k$ components most
responsible for any given input. While effective on small models,
APD scales poorly: its memory cost grows with the number of components,
as each component has the same dimensionality as the original network,
and its top-$k$ attribution is sensitive to hyperparameter choice.

SPD \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition} addresses these limitations by enforcing a rank one structure on each component, and replacing top-$k$ attribution with a learned causal importance function. Formally, each weight matrix $W^l$ is decomposed into a sum of $C$ rank one subcomponents:
\begin{equation}
W^{l}_{i,j} \approx \sum_{c=1}^{C} U^{l}_{i,c} V^{l}_{c,j}, 
\quad \text{where} \quad U^l \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times C}, \, V^l \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_{\text{in}}}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is a hyperparameter, for the number of subcomponents for each layer, $l$ indexes the layer and $i,j$ are the hidden indices.
The subcomponent count, $C$, may be chosen to be larger than both $d_{\text{in}}$ and $d_{\text{out}}$, enabling the method to learn features in superposition \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}.

To enforce the properties above, three losses are introduced, which are optimized jointly.
Faithfulness is enforced through a mean squared error loss between the original network's weights and the sum of subcomponents:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{faithfulness}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{i,j} \left( W_{i,j}^{l} - \sum_{c=1}^{C} U^{l}_{i,c}V^{l}_{c,j} \right)^{2}  
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the total parameter count in the target model.

Optimization for simplicity and minimality is achieved through a
stochastic masking procedure. Intuitively, if a subcomponent is not
necessary for reconstruction, it can be sampled to zero without loss
of faithfulness. A causal importance function
$\Gamma: \mathcal{X} \to [0,1]^{C \times L}$ is trained to learn the
lower bound of the ablation. In SPD, $\Gamma$ is a set
of independent MLPs $\gamma^l_c$ that each take a layer's activation
and output a scalar importance for the corresponding subcomponent:
\begin{equation}
g^{l}_{c}(x) = \Gamma^l_c(x) = \sigma_{H}(\gamma^{l}_{c}(h^{l}_{c}(x))), \qquad h^{l}_{c}(x) = \vec{V}_{c}^{l\top} \vec{a}^l(x)
\end{equation}
here $\sigma_{H}$ is a hard sigmoid and $\vec{a}^l(x)$ is the
activation vector of layer $l$. $g_c^{l}(x)$ serves as the lower bound for how much a subcomponent can be masked. With $r^l_{c} \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$ sampled per subcomponent, the masked weight matrix $W^{'l}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    m_{c}^l(x, r) &= g^{l}_{c}(x) + \bigl(1 - g^{l}_{c}(x)\bigr)\, r^l_{c}, \\
    W'^{l}_{i,j}(x, r)  &= \sum_{c=1}^{C} \vec{U}_{i,c}^{l}\, m_{c}^l(x, r)\, \vec{V}_{c,j}^{l},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These masked matrices are trained to reconstruct the original network's output.
\begin{equation}
    f\!\left(x \,\big|\, \{W'^{l}(x, r)\}_{l=1}^{L}\right)
    \approx
    f\!\left(x \,\big|\, \{W^{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}\right).
\end{equation}
This is done through both a layer-wise and a global reconstruction loss. The layer-wise version is necessary to provide a stronger training signal as randomly masking all subcomponents at the same time can be too noisy to learn from.

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{stochastic-recon}}
      &= \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S}
         D\!\left(f\bigl(x \mid W'(x, r^{s})\bigr),\; f(x \mid W)\right), \\
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{stochastic-recon-layer}}
      &= \frac{1}{LS} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \sum_{s=1}^{S}
         D\!\left(f\bigl(x \mid W^{1}, \dots, W'^{\,l}(x, r^{(s)}), \dots, W^{L}\bigr),\;
                  f(x \mid W)\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is a task-specific divergence measure, such as MSE or KL divergence.

Lastly, to enforce minimality, the importance function
is trained to activate only the subcomponents
required to reproduce the target model's output,
leaving the rest inactive. The loss balances
reconstruction accuracy against the number of
active subcomponents, creating a tradeoff between
faithfulness and minimality.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{minimality}}
      = \sum_{l=1}^{L} \sum_{c=1}^{C} \bigl(g^{l}_{c}(x)\bigr)^{p},
      \qquad p > 0.
\end{equation}
\label{spd_minimality}

The full SPD objective is a weighted sum of these terms:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SPD}}
      = \mathcal{L}_{\text{faithfulness}}
      + \beta_{1}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{stochastic-recon}}
      + \beta_{2}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{stochastic-recon-layer}}
      + \beta_{3}\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{minimality}}.
\end{equation}


\subsection{Irreducibility and the natural unit of decomposition}
\label{sec:irreducibility}
A growing body of work has identified irreducible
multi-dimensional features in trained models,
in which concepts are encoded in higher-dimensional
subspaces that can not be decomposed into independent
one-dimensional features
\autocite{engels2025languagemodelfeaturesonedimensionally}.
This has prompted a redefinition of the superposition
hypothesis, which originally took features to be sparse
and one-dimensional, to allow features that are
irreducible and may span more than a single direction.

\textbf{Definition: Multi-dimensional superposition hypothesis}\\
Every hidden
state $x_{i,l}(t)$ can be written as a sum of feature contributions
\begin{equation}
    x_{i,l}(t) = \sum_j V_j \, f_j(t),
\end{equation}
where each $f_j(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{f_j}}$ is an irreducible feature with
internal dimensionality $d_{f_j} \geq 1$, each $V_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{f_j}}$,
and $\| V_k^\top V_r \| < \delta$ for $k \neq r$.

Here an irreducible feature is one that can not be decomposed into two independent or mutually exclusive features, as defined below.
The original mixture case is self-contradicting, so it is restated as in \autocite{busch2025}.

\textbf{Definition: Reducibility} \\
A feature $f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$,
with $\mathcal{X}$ a subset of the input space, is reducible into features
$G:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}$ and $H:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-k}$, with
$k, d-k > 0$, if there exists an affine transformation
\begin{equation}
    Rf + v = \binom{G}{H}
\end{equation}
for some orthonormal $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and vector
$v \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, where one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0em}
    \item \textit{Independent}: $P(G \in A_{G}, H \in A_{H}) = P(G \in A_{G})\,P(H \in A_{H})$
    \item \textit{Mixture of mutually exclusive features}: $P(G \neq 0, H \neq 0) = 0$
\end{itemize}
A feature that satisfies neither is irreducible.

Reducibility and the multi-dimensional superposition hypothesis
jointly define the internal structure of a feature, and
when it is in its smallest form. They do not, however,
define what the features are in the first place, nor how they
should be extracted from the network's parameters. SPD addresses
this, but in its original form recovers only rank one
subcomponents and can not express the multi-dimensional features
these definitions require.
This gap is what motivates adapting SPD to higher rank.
Rather than clustering rank one subcomponents post-hoc,
the rank one subcomponents are replaced with rank-$k$
subcomponents, so that multi-dimensional features can be represented
directly during training. This restructured formulation
is introduced in Section~\ref{sec:rank-k-attention}.

\subsection{Relational SPD (RSPD)}
\label{sec:rank-k-attention}
In the original SPD paper, the subcomponents are constructed as rank one matrices,
through an outer product of two vectors. As motivated in
Section~\ref{sec:irreducibility}, a rank one subcomponent can not
represent an irreducible multi-dimensional feature, forcing it to either
 be split across multiple subcomponents or approximated by a single one at
the cost of reconstruction. To allow such features to be captured during training,
the pairs of vectors are replaced with pairs of matrices.
\begin{equation}
W^{l}_{i,j} \approx \sum_{c=1}^{C} \sum_{r=1}^{k} U^{l}_{c,i,r} V^{l}_{c,r,j},
\quad \text{where} \quad U^l \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times d_{\text{out}} \times k}, \, V^l \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times k \times  d_{\text{in}}}
\end{equation}
Each subcomponent, denoted $W^{l}_{c} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$
with entries $W^{l}_{c,[i,j]} = \sum_{r=1}^{k} U^{l}_{c,i,r} V^{l}_{c,r,j}$, has rank
at most $k$, recovering the original SPD definition when $k=1$. As in the
multi-dimensional superposition hypothesis, $k$ is an upper bound on each
subcomponent's representational capacity; the effective rank may be lower.

In SPD, each subcomponent's importance is computed independently through an MLP followed by a sigmoid nonlinearity,
allowing multiple subcomponents to be active for a given input and thus distribute a
feature across them, even when it is irreducible. Likewise, the minimality loss does not translate to the rank-$k$ setting, where each subcomponent can represent multiple feature directions:
it imposes little penalty for distributing a feature across multiple subcomponents,
nor for a single subcomponent to encode parts of multiple features. (Section \ref{spd_minimality})

To address this, the causal importance function is redefined to maximize the conditional probability, that a given subcomponent $U_{c}^{l}V^{l}_{c}$, is responsible for a given feature, conditioned on the activations of all subcomponents in the same layer.
\begin{equation}
    g^l_{c}(x) = p\!\left(W^l_c \mid \{h^l_{c'}(x)\}^{C}_{c'=1}\right), \quad h^l_c(x) = {V^l_c}^{\top} \vec{a}^l(x) \in \mathbb{R}^k
\end{equation}
here $\vec{a}^l(x)$ is the activation vector of layer $l$ and C is the number of subcomponents in layer $l$, $g^{l}_{c}(x)$ the causal importance value and $h_{c}^l$ the input to the causal importance function.

The conditional importance is computed using a standard transformer block following the architecture of \autocite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. For each layer $l$,
the read activations \(h_c^l(x) \in \mathbb{R}^k\) of the \(C\) subcomponents are concatenated into a matrix \(H^l(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times k}\),
with no positional encoding.
These activations are projected into the transformer residual stream through a learned linear projection. The resulting representations are then processed by a standard transformer block consisting of a single attention head,
a feed-forward network, residual connections, and layer normalization.

Finally, each subcomponent representation is projected down to a single scalar, and a Softmax over all subcomponents is computed.
This produces a normalized assignment over subcomponents, $\sum_{c=1}^{C}g^l_c(x) = 1$,
causing the original minimality loss to become degenerate, as each layer contributes a constant value independent of the model state.
Instead, it is replaced
with entropy, to pressure the assignment to concentrate on as few subcomponents as possible. 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{minimality}} = - \frac{1}{|L|} \sum_{l=1}^{L} \sum_{c=1}^{C} g^l_c(x) \log g^l_c(x)
\end{equation}

For the sake of clarity, this reformulation is referred to as Relational Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (RSPD), to distinguish it from the original formulation.


An unfortunate limitation of this formulation is its computational cost. Computing
relational importance via a transformer over subcomponents scales
quadratically with the number of subcomponents compared to the linear cost of the original independent importance functions.

\subsection{Toy Model of Superposition}
\label{sec:tms-experiments}

The Toy Model of Superposition (TMS) \autocite{elhage2022toymodelssuperposition} is trained to reconstruct sparse vectors through a lower-dimensional bottleneck that forces the model to learn features in superposition. The architecture is a single-hidden-layer autoencoder with tied weights:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x} = \mathrm{ReLU}(W^{\top}Wx + b), \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}} \times d_{\text{in}}}, \quad d_{\text{in}} \gg d_{\text{hidden}}.
\end{equation}
TMS is studied in two regimes: one with independent inputs, reproducing the original SPD setup, and one with paired inputs that introduces statistical dependence. For both regimes, the rank of each subcomponent is kept at $1$, both because this is the ground-truth structure and to isolate the effect of RSPD.

To quantify how well the learned subcomponents recover the target model columns,
two metrics are computed across both regimes.
The mean max cosine similarity (MMCS) \autocite{cunningham2023takingfeatures}
measures directional alignment between each target column and its
best-matching learned subcomponent:

\begin{equation}
\text{MMCS}
=
\frac{1}{d_{\text{in}}}
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\text{in}}}
\max_{c}
\frac{
W_{c,[:,j]} \cdot W_{:,j}
}{
\left\| W_{c,[:,j]} \right\|_2 \, \left\| W_{:,j} \right\|_2
}
\end{equation}

where $c  \in \{1,\dots, C\}$ is the subcomponent index and
$j \in \{1, \ldots, d_{\text{in}}\}$ is the input index. A value of $1$ indicates
that every target column has a perfectly aligned subcomponent.

Apart from directional alignment, the strength of the alignment
is also measured, to detect feature shrinkage, a problem apparent
in both Sparse Autoencoders and Attribution-Based Parameter
Decomposition \autocite{jermyn2024tanhPenaltyDictionaryLearning,wright2024featureSuppressionSAEs,AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition}.
The mean $L_{2}$ ratio (ML2R) compares the $L_{2}$ norm of the best-aligned
subcomponent to that of the target model for each of its columns \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{ML2R}
=
\frac{1}{d_{\text{in}}}
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{\text{in}}}
\frac{
\left\| W_{\mathrm{mcs}(j),[:,j]} \right\|_2
}{
\left\| W_{:,j} \right\|_2
}
\end{equation}
where $\text{mcs(j)} = \arg \max_{c}$ of the MMCS expression for column $j$.
A ratio near $1$ indicates the target column $W_{:,j}$ is fully
captured by its best-aligned subcomponent.

\subsection{Toy Model of Compressed Computation}
\label{sec:tcc-experiment}
The Toy Model of Compressed Computation, already studied by SPD
\autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}, APD
\autocite{AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition}, and more recently by
\autocite{bhagat2025compressed}, is trained to compute the elementwise function
$y_i = x_i + \mathrm{ReLU}(x_i)$ through a residual MLP with a lower-dimensional
hidden representation.

The architecture consists of an embedding matrix $W_E$, an unembedding matrix
$W_U = W_E^\top$, and a residual MLP with input projection
$W_{\text{in}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}} \times d_{\text{emb}}}$ and output projection
$W_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{emb}} \times d_{\text{hidden}}}$. The input vectors are
sparse activations sampled uniformly over $[-1, 1]$, and the model is trained to
minimize the MSE between its output and the target function. For comparability with previous work, this experiment uses an input vector $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{100}$,
$d_{\text{hidden}} = 50$, rank $k=1$ matrices, $C = 200$ subcomponents, and an
embedding dimension $d_{\text{emb}} = 1000$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=15cm, height=5cm]{CCmodel.png}
    \caption{Architecture of The Toy Model of Compressed Computation. Picture taken from \autocite{bhagat2025compressed}}.
\end{figure}
A naive solution would assign each hidden neuron to one of the $100$ ReLU
functions, but this would perform poorly on the remaining computations. Instead, to
minimize the loss, the model approximates all $100$ computations jointly
\autocite{bhagat2025compressed}.

The ground truth for $W_{\text{in}}$ follows directly, where one subcomponent should be
causally important for one input, matching the independent
one-dimensional structure of the inputs. For $W_{\text{out}}$ it is less clear.
APD \autocite{AttributionBasedParameterDecomposition} initially expected a
monosemantic assignment, with each subcomponent responsible for one of the
$100$ ReLU computations, while SPD \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}
reports a polysemantic one, in which $50$ subcomponents jointly contribute to
all computations and the rest are pruned to near-zero norm,
which is most in line with the result of \autocite{bhagat2025compressed},
and is most likely the correct interpretation.

\subsubsection{Causal importance}
In its causal importance assignment, RSPD finds a middle ground between SPD and
APD. In $W_{\text{in}}$ it produces a one-hot assignment, matching the ground truth.
In  $W_{\text{out}}$ it
diverges from SPD, rather than assigning 50 subcomponents to
all computations at once, the importance is concentrated with some 
subcomponents being important for several at once.
The assignment therefore appears sparser than the computation
underneath it actually is. (Figure \ref{fig:cc_importance})
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=18cm]{softmax_CC.png}
    \caption{Causal importance values for RSPD on The Toy Model of Compressed Computation,
    with each input activated in isolation at magnitude $0.75$.
    The top heatmap is the first layer $W_{1}$ and the bottom
    heatmap is the second layer $W_{2}$, with rows corresponding to the subcomponent index and
    columns to the causal importance value. The subcomponents are permuted within each panel. The model is trained using one-hot sampling.}
    \label{fig:cc_importance}
\end{figure}

To examine whether this assignment reflects the underlying decomposition, the
neuron-level contributions of the subcomponents are compared to those of the
target model \autocite{stochasticparameterdecomposition}.

\begin{align}
    \text{NeuronContribution}_{\text{model}}(i) &= \left( W_{U[i,:]}W_{\text{out}} \right) \odot \left( W_{\text{in}}W_{E[:,i]} \right) \label{eq:neuroncontribution} \\
        \text{NeuronContribution}_{\text{subcomponent}}(i) &= \max_{c}
\left[ \left( W_{U[i,:]} \sum_{c'} W_{\text{out},c'} \right)
\odot \left( W_{\text{in},c}\, W_{E[:,i]} \right) \right] \label{eq:subcomponentcontribution}
\end{align}
where $W_{E[:,i]}$ and $W_{U[i,:]}$ are the $i$-th column and row of the embedding
and unembedding matrices, $c, c' \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ index subcomponents, and $\odot$
denotes elementwise multiplication. The term $W_{U[i,:]}W_{\text{out}}$ measures how
strongly the neurons contribute to the output, and $W_{\text{in}}W_{E[:,i]}$ how
strongly they are activated by column $i$. A neuron contributes to column $i$'s
computation only when both signals are strong; if either is small, its contribution
is negligible.

In this case, RSPD replicates the target model's structure. For
every input there is a subcomponent that projects through the same hidden neurons
as in the target model. This is accompanied, however, by some misalignment in
contribution strength. Despite the correct hidden-neuron assignment, the magnitudes
are over- or under-represented (Figure~\ref{fig:cc_contribution}). This is consistent
with the lower ML2R seen in TMS and paired TMS, and occurs even though the
faithfulness and reconstruction losses are near-zero.

    first ten input columns of $W_{in}$. The top row is computed from Equation~\eqref{eq:neuroncontribution},
    and the bottom row from Equation~\eqref{eq:subcomponentcontribution}.}
    \label{fig:cc_contribution}
\end{figure}

While the $100$ and $50$ causally important subcomponents of $W_{\text{in}}$ and $W_{\text{out}}$

More generally, this behaviour is likely related to the feature
shrinkage phenomenon previously observed in TMS.
The faithfulness objective is computed additively as the MSE between
the summed subcomponent reconstruction and the target weight matrix,
making it indifferent to how reconstruction mass is allocated
across subcomponents. Consequently, if the importance regularization only weakly
penalizes low-magnitude subcomponents, optimization may converge
to solutions in which a small set of dominant subcomponents carry
most of the semantic structure, while a long tail of weakly but relevantly active
subcomponents absorb the difference.

\subsection{Toy Model of Geometry}

In the paired TMS regime, even though input pairs were nominally two-dimensional, the model collapsed them onto a single direction,
so a rank-1 decomposition per feature was sufficient. To test the rank-$k$ extension of Section~\ref{sec:rank-k-attention},
a setting is needed where features can not collapse to a single direction.
This experiment generalizes the cube example from the introduction, where a model is trained to compute the volume of an $n$-dimensional simplex from its $n+1$ vertices in $\mathbb{R}^n$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Vol}(v_1, \ldots, v_{n+1}) = \frac{1}{n!} \left| \det\!\left( \begin{bmatrix} v_2 - v_1 & v_3 - v_1 & \cdots & v_{n+1} - v_1 \end{bmatrix} \right) \right|.
\end{equation}


To mirror the paired setup of Section~\ref{sec:tms-tied-experiment},
the model receives $K$ simplices as input, each sampled with probability $p$ and otherwise set to zero across all of its coordinates:
\begin{equation}
    f(x) = W_{out} \text{ReLU}(W_{in} x), \quad W_{in} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}} \times K n(n+1)}, \quad W_{out} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times d_{\text{hidden}}},
\end{equation}
here $n(n+1)$ is the total number of coordinates of a simplex in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $K$ is the number of simplices.
Concretely, for this experiment the simplices are triangles in $\mathbb{R}^2$, with $K = 3$ and $d_{\text{hidden}} = 150$.

Unlike TMS, $d_{\text{hidden}}$ is chosen to be much greater than the input dimension (here $d_{\text{in}}= 18$),
so that the model does not need to express features in superposition.
The intention being that the model assigns each simplex to a
separate subset of neurons, allowing the routing of information
from input to output to be studied in isolation, as the minimality
loss is applied at different strengths and as the rank
is increased.


\subsubsection{Target model structure}
To examine how the model computes the volume, its computation is split into
an input side and an output side, following Section~\ref{sec:tcc-experiment}.

The input vector $\vec{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{18}$ is divided into $K$ disjoint
groups, where $\mathcal{C}_g$ is the set of column indices belonging to simplex
$g$ and the groups jointly cover all input columns.

On the input side, the influence of simplex $g$ on hidden neuron $i$ is the
total weight from that simplex's columns into the neuron,
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{[i,g]} := \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_g} W_{\text{in},[i,j]}.
\end{equation}
Each $\rho_{[i,g]}$ is the portion of neuron $i$'s total input weight,
$\sum_{j} W_{\text{in},[i,j]}$, contributed by simplex $g$.
The contribution of neuron $i$ to output $o$ follows directly from its entry $W_{\text{out},[o,i]}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, height=5cm]{w1_w2_input_hidden_output_translation.png}

    hidden neuron $i$. Bottom: the output-side contribution $W_{\text{out},[k,i]}$ of each

    some group and $W_{\text{out},[o,i]} > 0.05$ for some output are shown. Neurons are

    \label{fig:simplex_target_structure}
\end{figure}

The target model learns a predominantly modular structure \footnote{make font larger}
(Figure~\ref{fig:simplex_target_structure}). Each simplex activates a distinct
subset of the hidden neurons, and those neurons,
contribute to that simplex's output.
Folding these responses into a simplex to output matrix, shows
an almost diagonal structure, with each simplex contributing predominantly to its own output.
(Figure~\ref{fig:simplex_group_output}).
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    $P_{g,:} = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big[\, W_{\text{out}}\,\mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{in}}\, x^{(g)}_n)\,\big]$
    is the mean output when only simplex $g$ is active, where each probe
    $x^{(g)}_n$ has the coordinates $\mathcal{C}_g$ of simplex $g$ sampled and all other

    \label{fig:simplex_group_output}
\end{figure}
The decomposition of the first layer, should therefore 
be expected to recover a set of subcomponents that
are only causally important for one simplex each.
For the output, it was initially unclear, whether
the model would learn to compute the volume of each simplex
through a shared set of neurons,
or whether it would assign each simplex its own set.
The figures above suggest the latter.

\subsubsection{Subcomponent structure}
To examine how the decomposition realizes this,
the same quantities are redefined over the subcomponents,
measuring how strongly they couple to one another across all hidden neurons.

Analogous to the definition above, the additive contribution of simplex $g$ to
hidden neuron $i$ through subcomponent $c$ is given by
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{c,[i,g]} := \sum_{j \in \mathcal{C}_g} W_{\text{in},c,[i,j]},
    \qquad
    \rho_{[i,g]} \approx \sum_{c=1}^{C} \rho_{c,[i,g]}.
\end{equation}
here, i,g are hidden indices, $\mathcal{C}_{g}$ the column indices of simplex $g$, and $c$ the subcomponent index.

The output side similarly to before is given directly. The contribution of subcomponent $c$ at
neuron $i$ to output $o$ is the weight entry $W_{\text{out},c,[o,i]}$, with
$\sum_{c=1}^{C} W_{\text{out},c,[o,i]} \approx W_{\text{out},[o,i]}$.

Finally, because subcomponents connect through multiple hidden neurons,
their total coupling across all of them is measured. The coupling matrix
$\Psi \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$ is the inner product, taken over the hidden
neurons, between the total input read by $c$ and the total output written by
$c'$ at each neuron.
\begin{equation}
    \Psi_{c',c} := \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{hidden}}}
    \left( \sum_{j=1}^{d_{\text{in}}}  W_{\text{in},c,[i,j]}  \right)
    \left( \sum_{k=1}^{d_{\text{out}}} W_{\text{out},c',[k,i]} \right).
\end{equation}
It is large when the hidden neurons that $c$ reads from the input are the same
neurons that $c'$ writes to the output from.
The form of $\Psi$ follows from composing the two subcomponents as a single
linear map. Ignoring the ReLU, the entry $\Psi_{c',c}$ equals the sum over all
entries of the matrix product $W_{\text{out},c'} W_{\text{in},c}$.

These three quantities together describe the routing through the decomposition. The input
contribution $\rho_{c,[i,g]}$ shows how each simplex group contributes to subcomponent $c$,
the output contribution $W_{\text{out},c',[k,i]}$ shows how subcomponent $c'$ contribute to each output,
and the coupling matrix $\Psi_{c',c}$ composes the two, measuring how
strongly a subcomponent $W_{\text{in},c}$ connects to a subcomponent $W_{\text{out},c'}$
through shared hidden neurons.


Unlike in the paired TMS model, a simplex's computation
is spread across a higher dimensional space,
and is not collapsible into a single direction, that
a single rank-1 subcomponent could capture.
Whether the decomposition method recovers the 
ground-truth structure inside a set of rank one subcomponents,
or a single higher dimensional subcomponent
that captures the entire simplex,
should depend on two factors: the dimensionality each
subcomponent is allowed to represent, and how strongly
the minimality loss penalizes spreading importance
across multiple subcomponents. As these parameters are
varied, the recovered decomposition should
change gradually: a subcomponent may capture progressively
larger fractions of a single simplex, converging towards
the ground-truth decomposition. Alternatively, the additional
capacity may be used to represent parts of multiple simplices
simultaneously, resulting in a decomposition that mixes otherwise
reducible computations.

To isolate the effect of the subcomponent rank from that of the minimality coefficient,
the two factors are varied one at a time. First, the rank is fixed at $k=1$, recovering the
original SPD formulation, and the minimality coefficient $\beta_3$ is varied to examine its
influence on the decomposition. Second, $\beta_3$ is fixed at a value that produced a clear
decomposition at $k=1$, and the rank is increased to examine it influences
the decomposition.


\subsubsection{Comparison of importance values across different minimality coefficients}
With the rank fixed at $k=1$, the minimality coefficient $\beta_3$ is
varied in order to compare how SPD and RSPD distribute importance
under different levels of sparsity pressure.
Since a rank-1 subcomponent
can not represent a full simplex, the focus lies on
how each method allocates importance across subcomponents, when
the capacity is insufficient.

In SPD, when the minimality coefficient is set to $\beta_3 = 10^{-3}$, the
minimality loss dominates and almost all importance is suppressed, so no usable
decomposition is recovered. As $\beta_3$ is relaxed the expected structure
emerges, clearest around $\beta_3 = 10^{-5}$. The coordinates of each simplex
become important for a distinct block of subcomponents. The recovered grouping
reproduces the hypothesized ground-truth decomposition at the level of
simplices. Within each group, however, importance over individual coordinates is
spread across multiple simplices.
For $\beta_3 = 10^{-6}$ and below, this grouping dissolves and individual
subcomponents become important for several simplices at once, mixing the
otherwise independent computations (Figure~\ref{fig:spd_minimality_sweep_condensed}).

For RSPD, the same sweep yields very different results. At $\beta_3 = 10^{-3}$
the decomposition finds a near-diagonal representation, with each subcomponent
causally important for a single coordinate of one simplex. Lowering $\beta_3$
gradually makes subcomponents causally important for multiple
coordinates of the same simplex, with some cross simplex importance.
(Figure~\ref{fig:softmax_minimality_sweep_condensed}).

In the second layer, the two methods diverge. For SPD at $\beta_3 = 10^{-5}$,
one subcomponent becomes causally important for each simplex, recovering the
hypothesized decomposition. For RSPD, the model instead recovers the alternative
decomposition hypothesized earlier, in which a single subcomponent is causally
important for all three simplices.

\subsubsection{Comparison of importance values across ranks at fixed minimality}
Increasing the rank raises each subcomponent's representational capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:rank-k-attention}), so a single higher-rank subcomponent could
in principle absorb an entire simplex. To isolate the effect of capacity, the
rank is swept with the minimality coefficient held fixed. Following the previous section, the minimality coefficient is fixed at $10^{-5}$
for SPD, where it minimizes the global loss, and similarly for RSPD at $10^{-3}$.

In SPD, the minimality loss does not consolidate each simplex into a
single subcomponent as the rank increases. Instead, as each subcomponent becomes
more expressive, the number of active subcomponents falls, and each becomes
causally important for multiple simplices in the first layer,
while in the second layer, the loss rewards an indifferent behaviour, with one
subcomponent responsible for one simplex each
(Figure~\ref{fig:spd_rank_minimality_sweep}).

For RSPD, the causal importance assignment does show the desired trend,
as the rank increases one subcomponent gradually becomes causally important for one simplex
in the input layer, while in the output layer, the same behaviour is seen as with the minimality sweep,
where one subcomponent becomes causally important for all three simplices, with the remaining subcomponents being inactive.
(Figure \ref{fig:softmax_rank_sweep_minimality}).

\section{Discussion}
In every experiment, both SPD and RSPD reached near-zero
faithfulness loss and reconstructed the layer outputs accurately.
The two methods nonetheless produced very different internal
structures. The central finding of this work is that, under
the modified RSPD formulation, a sparse causal importance
assignment does not imply a sparse underlying representation.

In The Toy Model of Compressed Computation,
the one-hot assignment suggested a monosemantic assignment,
yet at the neuron level it did not singlehandedly capture the full neuron contribution 
of the target model. In The Toy Model of Geometry,
the importance values likewise suggested a per-simplex assignment in the first layer, but
the routing showed subcomponents reading from multiple simplices at once.
Finally, in The Toy Model of Superposition, where SPD and RSPD produced the same one-hot importance
assignment, RSPD diverged on both MMCS and ML2R, recovering a rotated geometry with
residual magnitude spread across neighboring subcomponents.

It was initially hypothesized that normalizing the importance distribution would tie
it directly to the magnitude of each subcomponent, so that a subcomponent's predicted
importance and its share of the reconstruction would correlate. This did not hold
in practice. The cause is not
entirely clear, but it is hypothesized to relate to how entropy shapes
the subcomponent norms during training, where causally unimportant subcomponents
are not pruned to zero norm but remain residually active. 
This was surprising, since SPD's minimality term is also only
an indirect penalty on the norm, yet it still prunes
inactive subcomponents to near-zero. 
It can not, however, be ruled out that the effect stems from the interaction
of softmax and entropy, or that the method might find better success in a
setting where each subcomponent must reconstruct the output within every subset,
rather than only when all others are ablated,
as proposed in VPD. \autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd}

Whichever the cause, RSPD in its current form does not appear to be the
right tool for parameter decomposition. 
This is clearest in the compressed-computation model,
where computation cannot be attributed to single directions in
superposition and the ground truth aligns more closely with the basis
vectors of the hidden space \autocite{bhagat2025compressed,stochasticparameterdecomposition}.
Softmax with entropy is therefore likely better suited to settings
with a binary measure of importance, rather than to settings
where the right target is likely
a continuum between the two. \autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd}

In the Toy Model of Geometry, SPD managed to find a clustered representation over
multiple subcomponents, that jointly captured the features of each simplex.
If the rank was increased, the clustered structure did not consolidate into a single subcomponent,
but rather the additional capacity was used to give subcomponents shared ownership over multiple simplices.
If SPD is to recover these features, then
future work should investigate alternative designs that enforce the necessary structural pressure.

The rank of the subcomponents connects to a broader ambiguity about what structure
parameter decomposition is meant to recover. In SPD the rank one constraint is
intended to enforce the simplicity of a mechanism, but even rank one
subcomponents can carry features of different nominal complexity,
depending on the target model's training. In TMS, both methods would capture
either a single input or a pair of inputs, depending on how these were projected in
the hidden representation of the target model. When a pair's activations are correlated, the two are
aligned along a common direction and captured by a single rank one subcomponent.
In that sense one decomposition is strictly finer than the other.
The same pattern appears in VPD, where a toy model implements multiple collinear
circuits that are captured by a single rank 1 subcomponent,
that becomes causally important for all of them \autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd}.
It is hypothesized that this phenomenon would also translate to higher ranks,
where a single subcomponent that spans a higher dimensional subspace would,
subsequently also capture multiple features, that would lie in its subspace,
and therefore not incentivize the model to split them further.
Therefore, choosing whether to increase the rank is not a trivial decision,
and future work should investigate how to determine the right rank for a given setting,
and how to design methods that can recover the desired structure at higher ranks.

\section{Limitations and future work}
Beyond the limitations already noted, this
work has several main shortcomings.
Firstly, the comparison between SPD and RSPD conflates two changes at once.
SPD computes each subcomponent's
importance independently with a small MLP, whereas
the architecture used here computes it relationally,
through a transformer block with softmax over subcomponent
activations. The observed differences therefore cannot
be attributed to either change in isolation. It is however,
likely to be related to the softmax, since
concurrent work has achieved good results, with a transformer
based importance assignment \autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd}.

From the perspective of irreducibility, the experiments
do not examine whether a subcomponent is 
reducible or could be separated into even simpler subcomponents.
Further work should investigate how to examine this automatically,
for instance by applying the algorithms of
\autocite{engels2025languagemodelfeaturesonedimensionally}.
Additionally further work could investigate how 
reducibility is best attributed to parameter decomposition.
For example, if the reducibility conditions 
could be applied to separate subcomponents, that
contain two collinear features, into two
subcomponents that each capture one of the features.
\autocite{bushnaq2026interpreting_new_spd}

Finally, every setting studied here is a toy model with
a known ground-truth decomposition. The
architecture has not been tested on larger models,
where the true decomposition is unknown, which is
ultimately where a parameter decomposition method must prove useful.
This is compounded by a strong sensitivity to hyperparameters,
in particular the minimality coefficient and the subcomponent rank,
which were tuned against the known ground truth.
